Projects

Seven projects, five kinds of brain data

Each project below starts from a public research dataset. I rebuild the analysis myself, check it for the mistakes that usually make these numbers look better than they are, and publish the code and a working demo alongside the write-up. Two of the projects are mirrored on a second domain, which is why nine links point to seven projects.

01

CorticalTap

ECoG

I pointed a next word predictor, the kind used for text, at 735 electrodes sitting on the brain surface of 8 epilepsy patients. It was trained to predict movement from a tapping game. Then I built the tests that proved my own headline number wrong.

Why I built it: everyone assumes a bigger, fancier model wins. I wanted to actually check that against the simplest possible baseline, in public.

plain logistic regression 0.879 · transformer 0.858 · training on other patients made it worse in all 8 patients, not better

OpenNeuro ds005931, Wayne State University, CC0

02

Octaves in the Visual Cortex

EEG, SSVEP

Eight dry electrodes on the back of the head can tell which of four flickering rates someone is looking at. That is the core of an interface that lets a person who cannot speak or move pick an answer just by looking at it. The standard method looked fine until I checked what it was actually answering.

Why I built it: this is the same signal used in real assistive spellers for people with paralysis. I wanted to know if the textbook method people cite actually holds up on real hardware.

textbook method 59% · fixed to read the whole spectrum 83.9% · live, given two seconds 99.4%

OpenNeuro ds004745, CC0

03

Reading Walking Speed From the Scalp

EEG + EMG

EEG and leg muscle sensors from 58 people walking at three speeds. I used it to drive a simulated wheelchair speed control. Brain signal alone needs about twelve seconds to settle on an answer. Add the muscle sensors and it takes three.

Why I built it: this is directly related to the peripheral nerve health idea I am developing. I wanted to see for myself how much a muscle sensor adds over brain signal alone.

random split 92.3% (means almost nothing) · held out person, brain only 52.8% · brain and muscle together 97.6%

PhysioNet, Katmah et al. 2026, CC BY 4.0

04

NeuroSpeech

ECoG, speech motor cortex

Reads sentences from 256 electrodes in the speech motor cortex of a man with ALS, using data Stanford released in 2024. I also looked at what the model's mistakes say about how the brain plans mouth movement.

Why I built it: a short video called this kind of work telepathy, and that word is wrong in a way that matters. Part of the write-up is about what this technology actually is and is not.

word error rate 17.08% · gets the whole sentence right 37.2% of the time · built from 10,948 attempted sentences

Card et al. 2024, NEJM, Dryad 10.5061/dryad.dncjsxm85

05

neuroswitch

fMRI, nerve injury still running

Does the brain reorganize after someone loses the use of a hand to nerve damage, not brain damage. fMRI from 25 patients and 46 people with no injury, recorded while they drew with each hand. I built four separate tests meant to prove the result wrong before I would believe it.

Why I built it: this question sits right next to the nerve health work I want to build a company around. If the brain does adapt after nerve injury, that changes what a monitoring device should actually be looking for.

the basic hand effect showed up in 19 of 19 people checked so far · 17 of about 66 people processed

OpenNeuro ds008162, Washington University in St. Louis, CC0

06

SliceWise

MRI, Alzheimer's

A model that reads Alzheimer's stage from one MRI slice scored 99 percent, until I noticed the same person's scans were landing on both sides of the train and test split. Once I fixed that, most of the accuracy was gone. I also built a general checker anyone can use to find the same mistake in their own dataset.

Why I built it: a video made this look like a weekend project. It is, but the honest version of it is a much smaller result than the video showed, and I wanted to show exactly why.

split by slice 98.9% · split by patient 33.7% · dementia vs none, judged per patient 0.94 AUC

OASIS, Washington University in St. Louis

07

MyoDecode

EMG, hand gestures

Reads five hand gestures from four muscle sensors on the forearm in under half a second. A method published in 1993 beats a modern neural network here. Most of the accuracy people report for this kind of task turns out to come from how the data gets split, not from the model.

Why I built it: muscle sensors are the other half of the nerve health idea. I wanted to know how few sensors and how little time I could get away with and still read a gesture reliably.

leaky split 98.7% · tested on a person it had never seen 93.4% · still works with one sensor 80.6%

8 subject EMG set, leakage checked against Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023
