Ten projects,each one checked

Each project starts from a public dataset. I rebuild the analysis, look for the mistakes that make these numbers look better than they are, and publish the code and a working demo with the write-up.

CorticalTap

ECoG, recordings from the surface of the brain

I pointed a next word predictor, the kind used for text, at 735 electrodes sitting on the brain surface of 8 epilepsy patients. It was trained to predict movement from a tapping game. Then I built the tests that proved my own headline number wrong.

Why I built it: everyone assumes a bigger, fancier model wins. I wanted to check that against the simplest possible baseline, in public.

  • 0.879plain logistic regression
  • 0.858transformer
  • 8 of 8patients where training on other patients made things worse, not better

OpenNeuro ds005931, Wayne State University, CC0

Octaves in the Visual Cortex

EEG, steady-state visual evoked potentials

Eight dry electrodes on the back of the head can tell which of four flickering rates someone is looking at. That is the core of an interface that lets a person who cannot speak or move pick an answer just by looking at it. The standard method looked fine until I checked what it was actually answering.

Why I built it: this is the same signal used in real assistive spellers for people with paralysis. I wanted to know if the textbook method people cite actually holds up on real hardware.

  • 59%textbook method
  • 83.9%fixed to read the whole spectrum
  • 99.4%live, given two seconds to settle

OpenNeuro ds004745, CC0

Reading walking speed from the scalp

EEG and EMG, 58 people walking at three speeds

EEG and leg muscle sensors from 58 people walking at three speeds. I used it to drive a simulated wheelchair speed control. Brain signal alone needs about twelve seconds to settle on an answer. Add the muscle sensors and it takes three.

Why I built it: this is directly related to the peripheral nerve health idea I am developing. I wanted to see for myself how much a muscle sensor adds over brain signal alone.

  • 92.3%random split, which means almost nothing
  • 52.8%held-out person, brain only
  • 97.6%brain and muscle together

PhysioNet, Katmah et al. 2026, CC BY 4.0

NeuroSpeech

ECoG, speech motor cortex

Reads sentences from 256 electrodes in the speech motor cortex of a man with ALS, using data Stanford released in 2024. I also looked at what the model's mistakes say about how the brain plans mouth movement.

Why I built it: a short video called this kind of work telepathy, and that word is wrong in a way that matters. Part of the write-up is about what this technology actually is and is not.

  • 17.08%word error rate
  • 37.2%of sentences exactly right
  • 10,948attempted sentences in the data

Card et al. 2024, NEJM, Dryad 10.5061/dryad.dncjsxm85

neuroswitchstill running

fMRI, nerve injury

Does the brain reorganize after someone loses the use of a hand to nerve damage, not brain damage. fMRI from 25 patients and 46 people with no injury, recorded while they drew with each hand. I built four separate tests meant to prove the result wrong before I would believe it.

Why I built it: this question sits right next to the nerve health work I want to build a company around. If the brain does adapt after nerve injury, that changes what a monitoring device should actually be looking for.

  • 20 of 20people checked so far showed the basic hand effect
  • 18 of 66people processed, so every number will change

OpenNeuro ds008162, Washington University in St. Louis, CC0

SliceWise

MRI, Alzheimer's stage

A model that reads Alzheimer's stage from one MRI slice scored 99 percent, until I noticed the same person's scans were landing on both sides of the train and test split. Once I fixed that, most of the accuracy was gone. I also built a general checker anyone can use to find the same mistake in their own dataset.

Why I built it: a video made this look like a weekend project. It is, but the honest version of it is a much smaller result than the video showed, and I wanted to show exactly why.

  • 98.9%split by slice
  • 33.7%split by patient
  • 0.94AUC for dementia versus none, judged per patient

OASIS, Washington University in St. Louis

MyoDecode

EMG, hand gestures

Reads five hand gestures from four muscle sensors on the forearm in under half a second. A method published in 1993 beats a modern neural network here. Most of the accuracy people report for this kind of task turns out to come from how the data gets split, not from the model.

Why I built it: muscle sensors are the other half of the nerve health idea. I wanted to know how few sensors and how little time I could get away with and still read a gesture reliably.

  • 98.7%leaky split
  • 93.4%tested on a person it had never seen
  • 80.6%still works with one sensor

8 subject EMG set, leakage checked against Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023

EEG Artifact Scope

EEG, data quality

A tool that checks whether an EEG recording actually holds brain activity or just blinks, jaw clenches, and cable movement. Calibrated against 30 people wearing five headsets at once, each doing 20 cued blinks, so the same blink from the same person at the same instant was recorded five different ways.

Why I built it: most people check this by eye, which stops working once you have forty recordings and a deadline. I wanted a number you could put in a methods section instead of a guess. I found four separate bugs while building it. One scored every recording the same. One mistook a filter for muscle noise. One called broken electrodes healthy during heavy blinking. One penalized headsets just for having more electrodes to check. All four are written up.

  • 88 to 100%of cued blinks found, depending on the headset
  • 30 of 30people whose jaw clench scored worse than their own rest
  • 0 bytesof your recording uploaded. It runs in your browser

30 subject, 5 headset open dataset, cued blink and jaw clench protocol

Diabetes Risk Predictor

Clinical tables, the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset

A diabetes risk model trained on the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset, 768 women aged 21 and over, running entirely in your browser. The tutorial version is a linear support vector machine that scores 77.3% on the test rows but misses about half of the true cases. I added a logistic model with a sensitivity slider so you can see what catching more cases costs in false alarms.

Why I built it: as a teaching demo of how a clinical prediction model behaves, with its limits on the same page as its result.

  • 77.3%test accuracy of the tutorial SVM
  • 52%recall, so about half of true cases are missed
  • 614 and 154training and test rows

Pima Indians Diabetes Database (NIDDK), via a public mirror. Tutorial by Siddhardhan.

Chest X-ray Pneumonia Detector

Chest X-rays, pediatric

A VGG16 network fine-tuned on the Kaggle chest X-ray dataset, rebuilt from a tutorial with a proper validation split, class weighting and an honest evaluation. It runs in your browser with ONNX Runtime and shows a Grad-CAM map of where it looked. It is tuned to miss few pneumonia cases, so it raises a false alarm on roughly one in four normal X-rays.

Why I built it: to redo a popular tutorial with a proper validation split and report every number, including the false alarms.

  • 89.9%accuracy on the 624 image test set
  • 97.9%sensitivity, pneumonia caught
  • 76.5%specificity, normal cleared

Kermany et al., via Paul Mooney on Kaggle. Children aged 1 to 5 at one hospital in Guangzhou.
